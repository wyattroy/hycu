# The ethos behind the site

Wyatt, 2026-09-14, after reading site copy full of "arguing": *"i'm so confused why so much of your
content is combative. I want you to read my book and realize that THIS is my manifesto. THIS is the
ethos i want to imbue in my site -- not necessarily through its language, but this is how I move
through the world."*

Two sources, both his: the book **How to Change Institutions** (Wyatt Roy, illustrated by Julia
Mattis; PDF at `~/Documents/Classes/Year 2 Spring/Infrastructure/How To Change Institutions/`) and
the **care manifesto** from Pour, reproduced in full below.

**Not necessarily through its language.** The site does not borrow the book's vocabulary: no Zen,
no breathing, no "fellow traveler". The ethos shows in *stance*: how the copy describes the people
in a project, the problem, and our part in it.

---

## What the book says, as it applies to writing about our work

- **Collaboration over antagonism** (pp. 6-9). Assume everyone in an institution cares, and invite
  them to be their best. The book allows that some situations need a fight; it is written for the
  ones that don't. Our studies are those.
- **No opponents** (pp. 43, 74-76). People who disagree have lived different lives, and are no more
  wrong than we are. An administration, a client team, a competitor, a staff member, a user, even
  an AI tool is never cast as the other side.
- **Curiosity first** (pp. 15, 19, 44). "What do you care about? How can I help you?" and "What do
  you see that I am missing?" Findings are about what people care about and what they fear losing.
- **Findings as a gift** (pp. 23, 35). Wyatt gathered data and brought it to the people in power as
  a gift, and they reversed a decision in two days. Research is offered, not used to win.
- **Save the scraps** (pp. 21-22). Look for overlooked spaces, time, resources and people, so a
  change costs no one anything.
- **Reframe change as care for what people already value** (pp. 50-51, 64-65).
- **Feedback is a caring gift** (pp. 36, 78). Critique, review and audit exist to support, not to
  catch someone out.
- **Repair and admitting fault** (pp. 58, 60, 79-81). Saying where we were wrong, or where a tool
  stops working, is an act of care, not a caveat.
- **Flexibility** (p. 52). A brittle twig snaps; a supple sapling bends.

## The care manifesto (Pour), verbatim

> Like water, care takes many forms and is passed on from one form to another: rain becomes stream,
> becomes river, becomes lake, becomes cloud. Care begets care in a cycle that flows through states
> of need and surplus.
>
> Care requires the decentralization of the self; not to sacrifice oneself for another, instead to
> see in its place an ecology of interconnected concerns and fates.
>
> Care is more than an action. Care is a practice: the continuous, developing process of collecting
> and exploring new forms, techniques, skills, perspectives, and approaches for passing support
> onward. Care is not an outcome, nor a checklist – it is never completed. On small scales, care
> adapts; on large scales, care evolves.
>
> When care is consistently lost at the same point within a system, that point has been blocked from
> an adequate supply of care. When care is not given to those who are giving care, the care within
> the connected ecosystem deteriorates. When care depletes as it transitions, it is often not because
> it is being hoarded, but because it trickles into the cracks of a strained system.
>
> Care is not sustainable when it is freeflowing. A system that demands a vessel drain itself
> entirely to fill another introduces a state of drought. Systems that enable each of their parts to
> sustain a reserve of care are more resilient. We believe that designing a homeostatic care
> ecosystem is not a matter of generating more care, or pretending that less is needed; but of
> creating systems with durable, flexible, permeable passageways that allow for the adequate
> collection, intentional transfer, and resulting circulation of care.
>
> This means: no one's care is more important than another's.
>
> Care-givers must be given care.

## What that means for a sentence on the site

| Reads as combative | Reads as this ethos |
|---|---|
| people *arguing* from the same numbers | people *looking at* the same numbers |
| fight the administration, or work with it | framed as a choice the book makes, not a battle |
| a reviewer who *judges*, *catches*, *never certifies* | a fresh reviewer who *sees what the builder can't* |
| *nobody* had the numbers / *nobody* delivers care to them | say what was missing, without blaming anyone |
| a tool that *turns against* a worker | what the design protects, and for whom |
| competitors *unable to* / claims that *failed* | what each option does, held to the same standard |
| a gotcha ("reported as success") | the gap, described with care for the one who made it |

Absolutes (never, nobody, the only), win/lose verbs (argue, fight, beat, against, defeat), and
policing words (catch, fence, gate, judge) are the tells. They are not banned: the book itself says
"when to fight". Use one only when the sentence is genuinely about conflict, and then without taking
a side.

**Clarity is a separate standard.** Wyatt, same day: *"I'm realizing that I'm not that great a
writer, and I trained you to write like me, but my writing isn't very clear."* The personal
writing-style skill is not the standard for site copy. Short, plain, one idea per sentence.
