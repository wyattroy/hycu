Harvard Graduate School of Design · Design research · 2026

A third of the school had no desk. We showed the Dean why it mattered, and what to do about it, from free fixes to a new building.

Gund Hall was built in 1971 for 400 students. In spring 2026 it held 994. We spent a semester finding out what that does to the people inside it, and took the findings to the administration.

Starting point

Everyone had a story about space. Nobody had the numbers.

At the GSD, a desk is more than a desk. It's a piece of the public building that becomes yours for a semester, where you sit, make things, and run into people. Desks are allocated by program and by term, and everyone knew that some students went without. How many, for how long, and with what effect, was a matter of anecdote. The administration had opinions. So did the students. Neither side had data.

We set out to get it, and to present it directly to the people who allocate the space.

What we found

Most desks sat empty most of the time, and having one still mattered more than anything else.

We walked all four floors of Gund every four hours for seven days, logging occupancy with an iPad app we designed and built for the week: a floor plan you tap to say what any desk is being used for. We surveyed 162 students across every program. The app, the analysis, and the visualizations were all built with AI, which is how a team of three ran a seven-day study and still had time to argue about what it meant. The building is two and a half times over its designed capacity. There are 625 desks for 994 students, so 369 people, 37 percent of the school, have no desk at all. And during a normal week, most of the desks we counted were empty.

That looks like a case for taking desks away. The survey said the opposite. Having a desk predicts hours spent in the building, and hours in the building predict the things a design school is for: running into classmates without planning to, bumping into professors, feeling focused, feeling part of a community, feeling that you belong. The students without desks weren't just short of a table. They were outside the community.

Where we went

In a scarcity problem there are three moves. We proposed one of each.

When a resource is short you can increase it, reduce the number of people who need it, or use the scraps better. We brought the administration a concrete proposal for each. Kalana White proposed a design competition for Gund itself, turning the redesign of the school's own building into a live studio brief. Xavaar Quaranto modeled the school's economics and found it could offer free tuition if it reduced enrollment. And we looked for cheap, immediate interventions: things that could make life in the building a little better now, for nothing.

The findings went to the Dean and the administration as a set of data visualizations, presented in person, with the recommendations attached to the evidence that motivated them.

What changed

A school with a shared picture of its own problem.

Before this work, the space conversation at the GSD was a set of competing anecdotes. After it, everyone in the room was arguing from the same numbers, and the argument moved from whether the problem existed to which of the three moves to make. That is the outcome we work for in research: a situation the people inside it can finally see whole.
