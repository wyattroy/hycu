A palm-sized field guide to changing an institution from the inside, by working with it rather than against it.

How to Change Institutions is a small book for people inside schools, governments, and hospitals who want change that lasts. It came out of a year of doing exactly that inside one large university, and it is the method we bring to every institutional client. Illustrated by Julia Mattis.

Starting point

A fork in the road: fight the administration, or work with it.

Most writing about change assumes adversarial tactics: organize, pressure, win. Inside a design school that had outgrown its building, we spent a year trying to make the institution serve its students better, and kept arriving at the same choice. Part of us loves to rabble-rouse. The better part prefers to understand the person across the table, process our own frustration first, and build a relationship that outlasts the issue.

We chose the second path on purpose and treated it as an experiment: could collaboration produce more change than conflict, and could the method be written down simply enough for someone else to use?

What we found

Assume the people inside an institution care about each other, and invite them to act like it.

Every intervention that worked started as a conversation, became a collaboration, and ended as a friendship with someone on the faculty or staff. The ones that stalled were the ones where we showed up with a demand. The institution was not a wall to push against. It was a web of people, most of whom wanted the same things we did and had their own constraints we couldn't see from outside.

So the book's premise is a bet: that inside most institutions people mutually care, and that change comes from inviting them to be their best selves together rather than from forcing them to choose sides.

Where we went

Four parts, the plainest language we could manage, and an acknowledgements section a third of the book long.

The book covers how to notice what you want to change, how to work with institutional complexity instead of wishing it away, how to build coalitions one relationship at a time, and how to sustain momentum after the first meeting. It is written in the spirit of Thich Nhat Hanh's small books, How to Walk and How to Love: short, direct, and meant to be carried. The acknowledgements take up a third of the pages, because the ideas belong to the web of people who inspired them, and the book is honest about that.

What changed

It became the studio's operating manual.

The approach in the book is how we work with every institutional client now: a day in the room before any design, a scope the client can actually carry out, and a plan that treats their constraints as material rather than obstacles. The forgiveness platform and the Gund Hall research are both this method in practice.
